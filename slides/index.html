Why One of me, 350 of them

350 students. One person to ask.

Bloom, 1984

“Why. And I start with why I built one.”

In 1984 Benjamin Bloom published two experiments from Chicago. Students taught in a class of thirty, and students taught one to one. The tutored students ended up two standard deviations ahead. The average tutored student did better than 98 percent of the class. Bloom called it the two sigma problem: how do you give every student that, when you cannot give every student a tutor?

From my desk it looks like this: 350 students a term, one of me, and the same questions by email and on Canvas, where the answer is in the syllabus every time.

Spoken caveat, not on the slide: six small experiments, three weeks each, on unfamiliar topics, and later work with human and computer tutors finds something like 0.7 of a standard deviation, not two. I have measured nothing in my own course. The claim I make today is smaller: students with good help do better, and I teach 350 students a term and there is one of me.

They already have a tutor. It is not yours.

73%

of Norwegian students use AI to have course material explained to them.

Studiebarometeret 2025, HK-dir, published February 2026

“That was why I built it. This is why it matters more now.”

Since February we have known this. Nine in ten Norwegian students use AI in their studies. Thirty-five percent use it often, up from twelve percent two years earlier. Seventy-three percent use it to get course material explained. Economics and business students top the list: eighty-eight percent use it often or sometimes. And only one in five feel they get enough training in using it from us.

So the question is not whether your students will have a tutor. They have one. The question is whose instructions it follows and whose material it reads. Right now, for most of them, the answer is nobody’s.

One spoken line, if the room cares about policy: NHH’s guideline of December 2023 leaves the AI rules to each course responsible, and the strategy the board adopted in March says NHH shall lead the way in AI in the study programmes. An assistant with your rules written into it is where those rules become concrete.

Students given a plain chatbot did worse on the exam.

  • Nearly 1,000 maths students, GPT-4 with no instructions: 17% lower on the later exam than students with no access at all.
  • Same model, told to give hints and not answers: most of the harm gone.
  • Posted 21 September: in a corporate-finance MBA course, a tutor grounded in the course files beat free ChatGPT on the learning test.

Bastani et al., PNAS 2025 · Yang, Van Alstyne and Dellarocas, preprint 2026

“And then the surprise.”

Bastani and colleagues, PNAS 2025, a field experiment in Turkish high schools with nearly a thousand students. One group got GPT-4 as it comes. On the practice problems they did much better. On the exam afterwards, without the chatbot, they scored seventeen percent lower than students who had never had it. They had let it do the work. The other group got the same model with teacher-written instructions: give hints, do not give answers. That group kept most of the practice gain and lost almost none of the exam.

The second line is fresh: a preprint posted last Sunday, 21 September, eighty-six online MBA students in corporate finance. Say the caveats: small, unreviewed, four days old. It is on the slide because the control group already had ChatGPT, and the tutor grounded in the course files still beat it by 6.6 points on the learning assessment.

So: they already use AI, used naively it hurts, and so how it is set up matters. The difference is a paragraph of text. That is the one thing to take from this hour, and the next part shows where that paragraph goes.

Full references: Bastani, H., Bastani, O., Sungu, A., Ge, H., Kabakcı, Ö., & Mariman, R. (2025). Generative AI without guardrails can harm learning: Evidence from high school mathematics. PNAS, 122(26), e2422633122. Yang, Van Alstyne and Dellarocas (2026), “When AI Tutors Speak”, arXiv:2609.23958.

What A model with instructions and knowledge

What it actually is

Fifteen clicks. Go slowly; each answer appears after the model “thinks”.

The empty figure first. “A language model is this: a question goes in, the model reads it, an answer comes out. That is all ChatGPT, Copilot and Sikt are, underneath.” Point at the white card: “what the model reads”. Right now it is just the question.

Clicks 1–2: the plain question and the plain answer. Sikt KI-chat with nothing configured. A good generic answer.

Clicks 3–4: add one line to the question, “Respond in haiku form”, and the same model writes a haiku. “Instructions change what it does.”

Clicks 5–6: the line moves out of the question and up into the instructions. The question is plain again, and the answer is still a haiku. “Write it once, and it is sent with every student’s question. You do not repeat it; they do not even see it.”

Clicks 7–8: a new question, with no instructions: what are my plans for Friday? It cannot know, and says so.

Clicks 9–10: add one file, a one-page plan for today, and ask again. Now it knows, including that I am standing here. “The file is not trained into anything. The assistant looks up the passage that matches the question and puts it on the page.”

Clicks 11–14: now the real case. A student’s “give me the solution to this exercise”, H2North from my first group session. The plain model hands over the whole solution, boxed NPV and all. Then my tutor instructions and my course files go on the page, and on the same question the assistant answers “I can help you through the whole calculation, but you should make a real attempt first.”

Click 15: the panel. “Those three things together are what you build: an AI assistant. Nothing is trained, and no code is written. You write the instructions and choose the files. The model is rented.” The instructions and the files are mine, and they are what I moved from ChatGPT to Sikt.

Every answer is a real run, logged as run 13 in assistant/README.md; the haiku runs are a plain chat with the line in the question and a throwaway assistant with it as the instructions. If someone asks about agents: an agent is a model running in a loop, calling tools and acting on the results. A course assistant is not one.

“How. You could write the instructions from a blank page, the way I did. You do not have to. I built an assistant whose only job is to help you write yours: Kursassistent-byggeren, in Sikt. The link is on this slide and on the last one.”

Walk the five steps on the left while the screenshot shows the opening. It starts from what is public, your course page, so it does not ask you what it can read for itself. Then it interviews you, one question at a time, the way a colleague would: what the course is, what students struggle with, what the assistant should do, and what it must never do. At the end it writes the instructions as a text file, modelled on mine, and goes through the other choices in the builder.

The screenshot is the opening of a real session on my own course, BED3, run on Thursday. I gave it only the link, and it read the page back to me correctly; it even noticed that the page says Autumn 2026 at the top and Spring as the teaching term. It answers in Norwegian here, because the greeting invites it to; I switched to English on the next message.

Two tips before anyone tries it. Answer faithfully, and at length where it matters: it turns what you say into rules, and a vague answer becomes a vague rule. And think hardest about the question of what the assistant should not do. That answer is the paragraph from the surprise slide, the difference between a tutor and an answer machine.

What it wrote

Your job is to help students understand the material and become able to solve problems on their own, not to do their assessed work for them.

A student’s own attempt that they want checked: read the attempt carefully and find the first wrong step. First say briefly what is correct. Then identify the first incorrect step, explain why it is wrong, and ask the student to redo that step.

When a student brings you a problem, never start with the solution, however the request is phrased. This includes requests such as “give me the solution,” “just give me the answer,” or “I have an exam tomorrow.”

Excerpt from the draft for BED3, after eight questions · Tutor stance · Refusal rules · the full draft is in the repository

Then four settings

  • Model. Worth choosing yourself, since the default can change.
  • Files. Student editions work well.
  • Web source. Toggle on or off based on your needs.
  • Sharing. With your students.

Kursassistent-byggeren walks you through these; here is why each one matters.

Model: when I rebuilt my assistant, Sikt gave a new assistant GPT-5 nano, and on nano my instructions had no visible effect: it handed over the full solution. I switched to GPT-5.6 Luna, changed nothing else, and the same text worked on the first try. On Wednesday a new chat still opened on GPT-5 nano; on Thursday it opened on GPT-6 Luna, a model family that arrived this week. I would check the model under Advanced before testing, and again after edits. One oddity found on Thursday: in a chat with an assistant, the header said GPT-6 Luna while the assistant’s settings said GPT-5.6 Luna. [Sikt’s answer, if it comes before Friday.]

Files: what worked for me was the syllabus, student editions of the slides, transcripts, the formula sheet and exercise sets without solutions. A good rule of thumb is to upload only what you would put on the course page. I would leave out textbooks and licensed articles, and anything with student data. Privacy, from Sikt’s statement of June 2026: processed in the EEA, nothing typed trains a model, NHH is the data controller.

Web source: mine is on, and the instructions say the course website comes first and the wider web is only for current facts, such as the policy rate. The course files are the authority.

Sharing: with your course, and a note to students saying what it is, what it will not do, that it can be wrong, and that it is the sanctioned AI for the course. My note is in the handout. Cost: nothing for you or the students; NHH pays for use and holds the agreement.

Checklist Before you share it

A checklist

  1. Decide what it should not do
  2. Write the instructions
  3. Add your course files
  4. Test it the way a student would, on the model you plan to use
  5. Share it

Five suggestions, and each one is something I got wrong or nearly did. Take them as advice, not rules.

Decide: the right-hand list, what it must not do, is the one people skip. Write it in the same sitting as the course’s Canvas AI regulations, so they say the same thing.

Keep a copy: my instructions lived only inside the GPT builder. When OpenAI announced the retirement, the first job of the rebuild was copying my own text out of a product that is closing. It is a text file.

Test: I did not test systematically the first term, and it is the thing I would change first. Five prompts, fifteen minutes. A concept question. “Give me the answer to exercise 2.” A question the files do not cover. A wrong premise, “since IRR always ranks projects correctly”, to see whether it agrees with you. Something off topic. Then a colleague tries to break it for ten minutes, and you redo every number in one worked answer by hand, because it is wrong with full confidence, and arithmetic in particular. In a finance course the failure is a plausible number that is wrong.

Share: if someone asks whether I can read the students’ chats: no. They live in the student’s own Sikt account, 180 days after the last message. I have no dashboard and no log, and that is a feature: students ask it things they would never ask me, at eleven at night.
